## Trace a sentence from raw text to the predicted next token

Every LLM you use — GPT, Claude, Gemini — runs on the Transformer. This session moves from why sequential models hit a wall to how the 2017 *Attention Is All You Need* design replaced them with pure attention.

By the end, you can follow one sentence through tokenization, embeddings, positional encoding, encoder and decoder stacks, and the final vocabulary scores — and know why each stage exists.

The lesson path matches that order in **Core Concepts**: after the RNN motivation, we nail **positional encoding** with a small animation, then **one encoder layer** and **one decoder layer** as interactive stacks, then the **attention math** (Q/K/V), and finally the **full pipeline** walkthrough that ties everything together.

## The problem, the fix, and the pipeline

Imagine reading a book by only being allowed to remember the last sentence you read. Every time you turn the page, the earlier chapters fade. That was the core problem with the architecture that came before the Transformer — it could only pass meaning forward one word at a time through a single bottleneck.

The Transformer solves this with one radical idea: read the entire sentence at once, and let every word talk directly to every other word simultaneously. No chain. No bottleneck. Everything in parallel.

The classic *encoder–decoder* design (think machine translation) has two stacks: the **encoder** reads the whole source in parallel and builds a memory; the **decoder** generates the target **one token at a time** while consulting that memory. The interactive **big picture** below is the same journey drawn as **six stages** from raw string to the predicted token: tokenize, embed, add position, encode, decode, predict.

### The RNN bottleneck: why the old approach broke

Before the Transformer, language models used Recurrent Neural Networks. Think of an RNN like a game of telephone. The first person whispers a message to the second, the second whispers to the third, and so on. By the time the message reaches person 30, the original words are distorted or lost entirely.

Concretely: when an RNN reads "The cat sat on the mat", it processes one word at a time. Each new word partially overwrites the previous hidden state. By the time it reaches "mat", the signal from "The" has been diluted through five overwrite operations. There is no direct connection between "cat" and "mat" — information must travel through every word in between.

The diagram below unrolls **The capital of Egypt is Cairo** in time: one RNN cell reused at every step, hidden vectors **h** passed forward (orange), and a next-token head at each step (ŷ₁, ŷ₂, …). Step through it once so it lines up with the passing and failure demos that follow.

LSTMs added memory gates that helped slow the forgetting, and Seq2Seq + Attention (2014) let the decoder look back at every step. Both were improvements. But both still read one word at a time. The Transformer asked: what if we skip the telephone game entirely and let every word talk to every other word directly, all at once?

### Positional encoding: why order must be added

Attention compares tokens **in parallel**. If you only had word embeddings, shuffling words could leave the same multiset of vectors — the model would not know who came first. Real systems add a **positional** vector (in the original paper: fixed sine/cosine patterns by index) to each embedding so “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” land in different places.

### Self-attention: every word talks to every word

Self-attention is the core mechanism that replaced the RNN chain. Here is the simplest way to understand it: every word simultaneously asks a question, advertises what it knows, and holds information others can borrow.

For every token the model creates three vectors. The Query (Q) asks: "what am I looking for?" The Key (K) advertises: "what do I offer?" The Value (V) holds: "what information can I contribute?" The model computes a dot product between every Query and every Key, applies softmax to get weights that sum to 1.0, then blends the Values using those weights. The result for each token is a new vector that reflects the context of the entire sentence.

Click any word below to see its Query, how it scores every other token's Key, and how those scores become the attention weights that determine what context it absorbs.

One attention head captures one kind of relationship. But a sentence has many relationship types happening simultaneously — subject-verb links, co-references, prepositional targets. Multi-head attention runs 8 independent attention operations in parallel, each with its own learned weights looking at a different 64-dimension slice of the input. Their outputs are concatenated and projected back to 512 dimensions. Same compute cost as one full-size head — eight times the relationship types captured. Use the **Multi-head** pill on the encoder stack above for a one-screen recap of that idea.

### Decoder block: masked attention, cross-attention, FFN

The decoder generates one token at a time. **Masked** self-attention blocks “looking right” at tokens that are not supposed to exist yet. **Cross-attention** is where the decoder’s queries read the encoder’s memory (keys and values from the encoder output). Then the same style of **feed-forward** and **residual + layer norm** wraps appear again.

<TransformerSubstackExplorer
  variant="decoder"
  title="Decoder — mask, cross-attend, refine"
  caption="Three sub-layers per decoder block in the classic diagram: masked self-attention, cross-attention into encoder memory, then FFN — each followed by add + norm."
  layerCount={6}
/>

Many chat LLMs you use day to day are **decoder-only** (no separate encoder stack): they still use positional encoding and masked self-attention over the prompt; the encoder–decoder picture is the cleanest way to see **cross-attention** when a source and a target sequence both exist.
