{/* SECTION 1 - GOAL */}

<SessionSection id="goal" label="Goal">

## Understand how the model turns tokens into numbers it can reason over

Tokens are just integers -- the model cannot compare or cluster them as-is. This session shows how embeddings solve that problem by mapping every token to a point in space where similar meanings end up close together.

</SessionSection>

## Words become points in space

The embedding layer is a lookup table. Each token ID maps to a fixed list of numbers — called a vector. A real embedding model uses 768, 1536, or even 3072 numbers per token. These numbers are learned during training so that tokens used in similar contexts end up at nearby points. You do not design them — they emerge from predicting billions of next tokens.

The two steps below show how that process works: first you see the raw numbers, then you see what those numbers mean spatially.

### Similarity is cosine similarity

Once words are vectors, "how similar are these two things?" becomes a single calculation. The standard measure is **cosine similarity** — it looks at the angle between two vectors, not their raw distance. If two vectors point in the same direction, the angle between them is 0° and the cosine is 1.0 — identical meaning. If they point in completely different directions, the angle is 90° and the cosine is 0.0 — unrelated. A score of 0.85 means the vectors are nearly aligned — close in meaning even if the words look nothing alike.

This is why "car won't start" and "vehicle fails to turn on" score 0.91 — the vectors point almost the same direction. Keyword search sees zero character overlap and returns nothing.

### Embeddings capture meaning — keywords only match characters

A keyword search for "car won't start" finds nothing if the support doc says
"vehicle fails to turn on". The words don't overlap. An embedding search finds
it instantly — because both phrases map to nearby points in vector space.

Embeddings encode what a sentence means, not which characters it contains.
